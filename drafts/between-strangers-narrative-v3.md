# Between Strangers — Narrative Draft v3

**One-line pitch:** Making an invisible social relation legible — without turning interpretation into a system verdict.

**Reading structure:** seven web chapters. The thirteen internal beats still exist as the writing structure (see appendix); readers should never notice them.

## Chapter 01 — A Glance That Leaves No Trace

### Scene

An underground corridor. You are walking — watching the floor, keeping pace, thinking about the day ahead. A stranger’s gaze lands on you for two seconds. Or it doesn’t. If it did: did you notice? If you noticed: did it mean anything?

What just happened — and what could a design say about it?

### The design problem

Public-space gaze is often brief, backgrounded, embedded in walking, and difficult to describe as a stable event. Yet it can reorganize attention, interpersonal boundaries, and possible interaction. Something may have happened — it simply left no legible trace.

Two ways to fail frame the problem.

The first is to draw the meaning: a force striking a body, a spotlight on a person. These images do not represent the experience; they replace it with a verdict. Someone felt pressure — so the system shows pressure as a fact, and with it a threat, an expectation, a response the person never chose.

The second is to stop at findings. Interviews produce themes, needs, and implications that describe the experience beautifully and never say what to do with it. The description stays on the page; the design stays unstarted.

Both fail at the same point: they skip the relations.

The challenge was not to invent a symbol for “being looked at.” It was to find out which parts of this subtle experience could be reconstructed as relations a designer could work with — before deciding how those relations should look or behave.

**Four states a design must not conflate.**

1. A gaze occurred — someone’s eyes were directed toward you in the world.
2. A gaze was noticed — it entered your awareness as a cue, distinct from the background.
3. An exchange formed — you looked back, and two gazes met in a shared relation.
4. A meaning was construed — it came to mean something to someone: invitation, scrutiny, threat, or nothing at all.

Keeping these states distinct preserves room for interpretation. Collapsing them risks turning inference into judgment. The early direction crossed all four; the proposition stops at the third.

## Chapter 02 — I Drew the Answer Too Early

The project began outside the research: standing in a transfer corridor in Shanghai at rush hour — surrounded by people, feeling completely alone. In an atomized city, the social-emotional support that once came from stable relationships is fragmenting. Could the small, non-verbal encounters of public space become a real source of warmth — a moment of being seen?

That ambition was the reason to start, and it was too big to design. The project soon ran into a more basic problem: I did not yet have a designable account of the experience itself. I could not yet say, with enough precision, what entered awareness, what sustained an episode, what closed it, or which relations a design could legitimately work with. Before I had that account, I did what felt natural: I drew the answer.

I designed a first XR concept around three symbols: a large transparent bubble around each person; a small bubble materialized where two gazes met; and ground ripples that carried the echo of every interaction. The story wrote itself: the boundary could feel protective without being closed; a glance could become a shared object; a passing encounter could leave the space a little warmer.

*[three images: boundary / gaze object / ripples]*

It had warmth, and it had a ceiling. Each symbol was a verdict: *this boundary is permeable, this gaze is a connection, this interaction charges you up.* A heartbeat pulse when eyes met, and energy absorbed on parting, turned subtle relational states into emotional judgments the system had no evidence — and no right — to make. And nothing in the design could be traced back to what the experience actually supported.

**The form survived. The reasoning behind it did not.**

The membrane-and-bubble family returns in the final proposition — but only because it was re-derived, element by element, from what the experience itself could support. What follows is that re-derivation.

## Chapter 03 — Entering the Experience

To find out what a two-second glance actually is, I did not ask people what they thought about strangers. I brought participants into a first-person walking scenario — a five-minute video shot in an underground pedestrian passage in Shanghai — then asked them to return to what they had noticed, and what they had not.

- 16 native Mandarin-speaking participants
- 13:02 hours of interviews
- 160,162 Chinese characters transcribed
- 40–60 minutes per session
- Every evidence unit received a unique ID, so every later design decision could be followed back to the account that supported it.

The gaze direction of people in the video was not measured. The material consists of situated descriptions, not recordings of real-time cognition.

### Two different jobs

The same material was asked to do two different things.

**Perceptual analysis — What allowed gaze to enter, or leave, experience?**

Walking and collision avoidance usually stayed primary. A person could become noticeable before their gaze was localized. Noticeability was linked to field of view, distraction, passage pressure, duration, and persistence. Looking away or passing closed the episode and returned attention to walking.

These findings became conditional requirements: make a gaze event available without demanding constant monitoring; distinguish a visible person from a gaze directed at you; show whether the relation is brief, continuing, or over; make withdrawal and return to baseline perceptible.

**Conceptual-metaphor analysis — What relations organized participants’ descriptions?**

Across different accounts, gaze was described as something that travelled, arrived, stayed, met another gaze, carried content, exerted pressure, crossed a boundary, illuminated a person, or exposed a watcher.

These expressions were not treated as ready-made design ideas. Each mapping was reconstructed into its underlying spatial and event relations, then checked against its evidence record.

## Chapter 04 — The Interviews Did Not Tell Me What to Draw

*(Full-page climax. One screen: the line. Next screen: “They gave me relations.” Then the three rejected translations.)*

This is the moment the project changed.

The two analyses produced functions and relations — not a visual form. A participant described a prolonged gaze as pressure; another as intrusion; another as being caught. Drawing pressure striking a body would preserve one construal, and in doing so it would turn a situated interpretation into a judgment made by the system. That is exactly the failure the early direction had already made, now visible from the other side.

| Situated account | Literal translation | Why it was rejected |
|---|---|---|
| Prolonged gaze was sometimes described through pressure. | A force could be shown acting directly on the person. | That image would present pressure, threat, and an expected response as facts rather than possible interpretations. |

The interviews gave the design relations, not images. That was the contribution: the relations could be reconstructed, selected, and reorganized before any form was chosen.

## Chapter 05 — From Evidence to Design

### What the evidence could support

For every audited metaphorical mapping, I separated the participant’s situated meaning from the more basic spatial or event relation that organized it. Compatible relations were then synthesized across records. The result describes what the evidence supports — not what I wanted the final imagery to look like.

- Gaze as moving, arriving, staying, leaving → directed path with origin, movement, arrival, persistence, withdrawal, restoration
- Two gazes meeting, holding, breaking → contact formation, maintenance, separation, possible recontact
- Private activity as a bounded space; prolonged nonreciprocal gaze entering it → conditional bounded-access branch — not a claim that every sustained gaze is intrusion
- Weight, depth, sharpness, pressure, content, illumination, exposure → attached qualities and conditional modules, not a universal sequence

*[figure: Evidence-Side Image-Schematic System]*

### Evidence became design

Four inputs, shown as small labels: **Evidence** · **Perceptual function** · **Design value** · **Situation**. Then three decisions, shown large:

**RETAIN** — Gaze as directed movement, contact, and withdrawal.
The object shows source, direction, arrival, persistence, and withdrawal.

**RELOCATE** — Arrival moves to the space between, not the body.
Endpoints leave the person; the object stops at a common relation position between the two people.

**CONSTRAIN** — Pressure and intrusion become limits, not states.
Objects stay outside both domains; sharpness stays low; no force acts on the person.

And three visible restraints — the decisions about what not to show:

**Interior without content** — objects keep a container interior; the system never fills, labels, or classifies it.

**No spotlight** — illumination and exposure are excluded; event changes appear in the relation, not on the person.

**Geometry without judgment** — “catching” is reduced to contact geometry; reciprocity shows without accountability or blame.

## Chapter 06 — One Relation System, Three Visual Carriers

### The behavior script, drawn

Six states, shown as a diagram — not a paragraph:

```
○        ○      Baseline — only the two domains remain.
○  ·→    ○      A gaze forms — an object appears at a boundary and travels between.
○  ·→ ·← ○      Return gaze — a second object appears from the opposite side.
○   ◎    ○      Contact — the two objects merge. Contact is present, nothing more.
○  ·←    ○      Withdrawal — the object splits; the withdrawing one returns and disappears.
○        ○      Restored — all objects vanish; the imagery returns to its baseline.
```

The system represents observable gaze relations and state changes. Meaning construal remains participant-level.

### Three carriers

Because the system lives at the relation level, the same script can wear different visual languages. Three candidates were developed from the same input: **Membrane–Bubble**, **Wireframe–Ring**, and **Field–Condensation**. Five external designers with XR-imagery backgrounds reviewed them independently, then together. Their review supported Membrane–Bubble for further development — it did not validate the method, the script, or any experiential effect.

*[carrier-comparison image]*

### The proposition: Membrane–Bubble

*[four membrane images: domain / emergence / object / contact]*

Transparent membranes around both people — symmetric, low-salience, real eyes always visible. Small bubbles as gaze objects, born at the boundary, traveling to a common position between the two people. Merge into a single relation bubble when gazes meet; a discrete change in weight marks contact. Split, return, and vanish on withdrawal; the baseline restores. The interior of every object stays empty — open to whatever meaning the participant gives it.

These images specify a visual proposition, not a finished XR experience. The complete transition sequence, sensing implementation, visual load while walking, situated interpretation, and social consequences still require prototyping and evaluation.

## Chapter 07 — What the Project Actually Produced

**Produced:** a traceable way to move from first-person accounts to design requirements; an evidence-side reconstruction; a documented set of projection decisions; a design-side behavior script; three visual carriers; a preferred proposition.

**Still open:** whether gaze can be sensed reliably; whether the imagery stays legible without disturbing walking; how participants interpret and act on it; what consequences it creates for people who did not choose to use the system.

The early direction visualized meanings. The proposition visualizes relations — and leaves meaning with the people. The contribution is not a finished visualization of gaze. It is a method for reconstructing subtle embodied social experience as designable relations before symbolic form is chosen: a way to make an invisible relation legible without deciding in advance what it means. Demonstrated here through a proposition that can now be built, contested, and revised.
