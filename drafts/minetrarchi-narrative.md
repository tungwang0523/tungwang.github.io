# Minetrarchi — Narrative Draft

**One-line pitch:** Turning architectural knowledge into rules of play — learning a building by making it, not by looking at it.

**Core idea:** The building is not an image. It is a sequence of relations that can be learned by making.

**Reading structure:** eight beats, mirroring the Between Strangers treatment (scene → problem → research turn → core move → why the platform → the system → the interface → boundary).

---

## 01 · Scene

I spent years of my childhood and adolescence building buildings in Minecraft — first East Asian classical structures, then modernist ones. At some point I stopped noticing that I had learned a great deal about architecture: how a column carries a beam, how a bracket set mediates between them, why a roof's weight has to go somewhere. I learned it with my hands, block by block, long before I could have named it.

That is the experience Minetrarchi is trying to give other people.

## 02 · The problem: heritage knowledge stays on the surface

Most popular encounters with historic buildings stop at the surface: dynasties, ornament, and photographic appearance. The structural logic — why the building stands, what holds it together, how it was made — stays out of reach.

Three barriers:

- **Knowledge stays on the surface** — what is memorable is often folklore, not structure.
- **Sites cannot always be entered** — preservation, repair and visitor management keep people out of the interiors and construction details.
- **Existing media explains but rarely lets you act** — books, documentaries and museums give information, but no room to assemble, fail, compare and rebuild.

## 03 · The research turn: interest was not the problem

Seventy questionnaires and ten depth interviews pointed to a different diagnosis.

The numbers that matter:

- **79%** accepted the common but inaccurate claim that traditional timber buildings are built without a single nail — memorable folklore replacing structural understanding.
- **63%** learned about traditional architecture through documentaries or popular-science programmes.
- **61%** through museums or exhibitions.
- **60%** had met access restrictions around historic buildings.

People were curious. They lacked a route from curiosity to usable understanding.

> “A game could let me understand the building by making it myself, rather than only reading an explanation.”

## 04 · The core move: knowledge becomes rules of play

The opportunity was not to explain buildings better. It was to turn architectural knowledge into a construction grammar — components, order, proportion and craft — that players assemble themselves.

Instead of treating a traditional building as a façade to recognize, the design lets players learn it by taking it apart and building it again. Guided assembly teaches; free creation extends.

## 05 · Why Minecraft

The platform is not decoration. Minecraft's grammar is the design:

- **Construction grammar** — blocks, modular components and spatial rules make structure something players can manipulate directly.
- **Low entry, high ceiling** — a familiar sandbox invites quick experimentation and still rewards long-term mastery.
- **A culture of circulation** — maps, mods, builds and tutorials already move through a large creative community; architectural assets can move the same way.

The proposal adapts rather than replaces Minecraft. The base game supplies movement, building and multiplayer conventions; Minetrarchi adds architectural components, guided tasks, knowledge feedback, assets, progression and a service layer.

## 06 · The system: two worlds, one learning loop

The game is organized around a guided world and an open world.

**Artisan Continent (工匠大陆)** — players enter role-based architectural tasks, follow construction guidance, receive explanations and collect reusable components. Difficulty progresses from simple regional buildings to complex palace architecture.

**Dawn Plain (晨曦之原)** — players use earned or exchanged assets in free construction, browse community work, trade resources and publish their own architectural worlds.

The loop: *enter a task → learn a component → assemble → receive feedback → complete and earn → build freely → share or exchange.*

The full system is six parts: Space (maps and physics), Tasks (story, episode and expert-authored challenges), Assets (components created, stored and exchanged), Social (co-building, events, sharing), Achievement (visible progress), and Peripheral (model export, fabrication, physical services).

## 07 · A tutorial is a construction sequence

The key design decision is that the tutorial is not a diagram to memorize — it is a sequence of situated decisions:

1. **Set the plinth** — read the footprint, position and proportion of the base.
2. **Mark the column grid** — place reference points before placing components.
3. **Raise columns correctly** — ghost guides show location and orientation; the system catches hollow or misassembled columns.
4. **Connect the frame** — beams and bracket sets are introduced as load-bearing relations, not decoration.
5. **Complete the roof** — the structural and enclosure sequence finishes across roughly fifteen guided steps.
6. **Use the knowledge** — the completed building and assets move into the free-building loop.

The interface holds game and knowledge together: immediate building guidance, architectural explanations, a miniature map, material inventory, task steps and light social presence — without splitting the player away from the act of making.

The same building system works across PC, touch and VR — not three separate products, but three ways to inhabit one structure.

## 08 · What this is, and what it isn't

Minetrarchi is a comprehensive concept design: research, game mechanics, visual and interface design, and a service layer — documented as a coherent system.

It is not yet a built and evaluated game. The construction sequence, the knowledge feedback, and the guided-to-free learning loop are designed and specified, but their actual learning effect — whether building with the hands genuinely converts curiosity into understanding better than the media it replaces — remains to be tested.

That is the honest boundary, and it is also the next question.
