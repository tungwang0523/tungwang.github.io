        Notes on Project Urania-Weltzeituhr: Prototype 0

        Urania World Clock, Prototype No. 0

        The first time I spent a night in Room 104 was to finish a model.

        I first saw the Urania World Clock in a shot from the film Goodbye Lenin!, where the Berlin TV Tower and the clock appeared side by side. They became my first visual impression of East Berlin and the GDR when I was still in primary school. Leaving aside the history of Germany's division and socialist East Germany, is there not something deeply romantic about this design: the names of places around the world engraved on a fixed ring, while twenty-four numbers shift with time among twenty-four faces, so that every marking on the dial can show the present hour?

        That film also gave me an entirely new picture of Germany. As a primary-school pupil who knew little beyond the neoclassical Brandenburg Gate, I discovered that Berlin also had such avant-garde structures as the TV Tower, the Palace of the Republic, and the World Clock. I could not help developing a strong interest in Germany, and especially in East Germany in the latter half of the twentieth century. In retrospect, those three buildings were probably my first impression of modernist design.

        Back to the Urania World Clock on my desk. People around me have long joked that one place is my first homeland and another my second, and over time I accepted the premise. For a boy from Inner Mongolia, what could be more natural than being at home everywhere, a citizen of the world? An ordinary clock on my desk was clearly no longer enough. With so many past and future homes, how could it show me what time it was in each of them? My mind naturally returned to that wonderfully romantic East German clock in Berlin's Alexanderplatz. So a Urania World Clock it would be.

        I searched Taobao and Xianyu first, but found no model of the clock for sale. Even across the whole German internet there appeared to be none, apart from an official collector's model in stainless steel costing more than RMB 60,000. The natural conclusion was to build one myself, and so Projekt Urania-Weltzeituhr began.

        In fact, I did not have to design it from scratch. A German 3D-printing enthusiast had already published on Thingiverse a carefully surveyed and modelled version of the Urania World Clock. All I needed to do was print the model, buy a silent twenty-four-hour clock movement, and assemble it. It was not nearly as troublesome as I had imagined.

        Why call the finished piece “Prototype No. 0”? Once the parts had been printed, I was far too eager to see the final form of this miniature clock, while the movement that would drive its time disc had not yet arrived. Wait another two or three days, perhaps longer, for a single movement? No. I could not even wait overnight. And so Prototype No. 0 was completed at about six o'clock on January 15, 2026. I really should have recorded the exact time.

        Since the dial could not move, the world disc needed to be turned to a suitable position. I first considered two options: set it to twenty-four in Greenwich Mean Time, which corresponds to 24 rather than 0 on the dial, or set it to the full hour nearest the moment of completion. I ultimately chose the first. But when placing the clock, I turned the East Asia face marked “Irkutsk · Ulaanbaatar · Beijing · Shanghai · Manila · Perth · Hong Kong · Kuala Lumpur · Singapore” toward myself. From my viewpoint, therefore, it reads eight o'clock Beijing time. The day begins in the morning. I am rarely awake at that hour, but setting the dial there feels auspicious, like a quiet prompt to get moving.
